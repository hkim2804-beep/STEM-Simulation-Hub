Why this matters (teacher notes)

A chain or cable hanging freely under its own weight settles into a catenary: y = a cosh((x − h)/a) + k. Because cosh(u) = (eu + e−u)/2, this is really just a combination of two exponential functions — a direct link to the exponential functions taught in Maths Methods, dressed up as a real physical shape students can pull on.

For a shallow sag, a parabola y = Ax2 + Bx + C is a close approximation — the app fits both models and reports each one's root-mean-square error against the simulated chain, so students can see numerically when the parabola stops being a good model, not just visually. This mirrors the common misconception that hanging cables are parabolas (they're only approximately parabolic, whereas a projectile's flight path actually is one).

Notice that changing Gravity changes how quickly the chain settles but not its resting shape — the equilibrium curve is fixed entirely by the anchor points and the chain's length. Turning on centre weight breaks this: a point load makes the true shape deviate from a catenary, and the fit error will visibly rise. That's a good prompt for discussing the assumptions built into any mathematical model.

In Challenge mode, students type their own candidate function and adjust a, b, c, h, k to chase the live chain shape before revealing the exact analytic fit — useful for a parameter-transformation lesson (shifts, stretches) using h and k as horizontal/vertical translations.
