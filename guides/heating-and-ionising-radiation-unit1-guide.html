This guide accompanies two linked interactive simulations built for QCAA Physics Unit 1. Each has three modules with adjustable parameters, live graphs and worked calculations — built to make relationships tangible that are hard to picture from a static diagram: how temperature behaves while a substance changes state, why radioactive decay is random for one atom but predictable for a billion, or where the energy in E = Δmc² actually goes.

How to use each module

Topic 1 — Heating Processes

1. Heating CurveModule 1

Choose water, ethanol, or build a custom substance by setting melting/boiling points and specific and latent heats. Set mass and heater power, then Play or scrub through time to watch temperature plateau during melting and boiling, with a live particle view showing why. Playback now runs past the plotted curve if left going, with the axes expanding to keep the marker in view.

2. Thermal EquilibriumModule 2

Set the material, mass and starting temperature of two bodies, then bring them into contact to watch them settle to a common final temperature — calculated live from conservation of energy.

3. Black-Body RadiationExtension

Drag temperature (300–12,000 K) to see total power and peak wavelength/colour change live, via Stefan's Law and Wien's Law. Goes beyond this topic's official subject matter (see alignment below) but reinforces radiation as an energy-transfer mechanism.

Topic 2 — Ionising Radiation & Nuclear Reactions

1. Radioactive DecayModule 1

Pick a real isotope (or set a custom half-life) and Play or scrub through time. A smooth theoretical curve (N = N₀(½)ⁿ) is plotted against the actual, randomly-timed fate of a sample of atoms — each atom's decay instant is fixed when the sample is built, so scrubbing always shows the same consistent history.

2. Radiation & MatterModule 2

Alpha, beta and gamma radiation shown together as three rays from a source. Switch between Penetration (paper/aluminium/lead shielding) and Fields (magnetic/electric) views, alongside a reference table of charge, mass and ionising power.

3. Nuclear ReactionsModule 3

Choose a decay (α, β⁻, β⁺ or γ) or a fission/fusion reaction to see its balanced nuclear equation, a live conservation check, and the energy released — computed from real nuclear masses via E = Δmc², in u, kg, J and MeV.

Notes for teachers: where to supplement

The gaps above are intentional — concepts better served by discussion or worked examples than by a slider-driven simulation.

  • Conduction, convection and radiation (Topic 1): qualitative heat-transfer mechanisms — the particle view supports the kinetic model but doesn't distinguish the three.
  • Mechanical work and efficiency (Topic 1): ΔU = Q + W and η = (output/input) × 100% — worked examples (a heat engine, a kettle) work better here than a simulation.
  • Strong nuclear force and stability (Topic 2): why the strong force wins over proton–proton repulsion at nuclear distances, and how neutron:proton ratio relates to stability — the simulation's isotopes (e.g. contrasting a stable choice with U-238) make good discussion prompts once introduced.
  • Artificial transmutation vs natural decay (Topic 2): implicit in the simulation (Module 1 = spontaneous; Module 3's fission/fusion must be induced) but not labelled — worth drawing out explicitly.
  • Binding energy per nucleon (Topic 2): a one-step calculation from the simulation's own numbers — divide the displayed E by the total nucleon count. The worksheet uses this to let students discover for themselves why fusion releases more energy per nucleon than fission.

Both simulations note their own simplifications in-page (no energy loss to surroundings, illustrative field-deflection curvature) — worth pointing out as a live example of how physics models trade realism for tractability.
